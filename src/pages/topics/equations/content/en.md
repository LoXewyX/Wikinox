# Equations

An **equation** is a mathematical equality containing one or more unknowns.

For example:

$$
2x+3=11
$$

The letter $x$ represents a value that we do not know yet. Solving an equation means finding the values of the unknown that make the equality true.

## Parts of an equation

An equation consists of two expressions separated by an equals sign:

$$
\text{left-hand side}=\text{right-hand side}
$$

For example, in

$$
2x+3=11
$$

- $2x+3$ is the **left-hand side**.
- $11$ is the **right-hand side**.
- $x$ is the **unknown**.
- $2$ is the **coefficient** of $x$.
- $3$ is the **constant term**.

The solution is the value of $x$ that makes both sides have the same value.

## What does it mean to solve an equation?

Solving an equation means finding its solutions.

Consider:

$$
2x+3=11
$$

Subtract $3$ from both sides:

$$
2x=8
$$

Divide both sides by $2$:

$$
x=4
$$

Therefore, the solution is:

$$
\boxed{x=4}
$$

We can check it by substituting $x=4$ into the original equation:

$$
2(4)+3=11
$$

$$
8+3=11
$$

The equality is true, so $x=4$ is a solution.

## Equivalence principle

When solving an equation, we can perform the same operation on both sides without changing its solutions.

For example:

$$
x+5=12
$$

We can subtract $5$ from both sides:

$$
x+5-5=12-5
$$

giving:

$$
x=7
$$

The most common operations are:

- adding or subtracting the same quantity from both sides;
- multiplying or dividing both sides by a non-zero quantity;
- simplifying equivalent expressions.

The fundamental idea is to preserve the equality.

## Linear equations

A **linear equation** is an equation in which the unknown appears with exponent $1$.

Its general form is:

$$
ax+b=0
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are numbers and $a\neq0$.

### Example

Solve:

$$
3x-7=8
$$

Add $7$ to both sides:

$$
3x=15
$$

Divide by $3$:

$$
x=5
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{x=5}
$$

## Equations with parentheses

Before isolating the unknown, we can expand parentheses.

For example:

$$
2(x+3)=14
$$

Apply the distributive property:

$$
2x+6=14
$$

Subtract $6$:

$$
2x=8
$$

Divide by $2$:

$$
x=4
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{x=4}
$$

## Equations with unknowns on both sides

The unknown can appear on both sides of an equality.

For example:

$$
5x+2=2x+14
$$

Subtract $2x$ from both sides:

$$
3x+2=14
$$

Subtract $2$:

$$
3x=12
$$

Divide by $3$:

$$
x=4
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{x=4}
$$

## Equations with fractions

When fractions appear, we can multiply the entire equation by the least common multiple of the denominators.

Consider:

$$
\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{3}=5
$$

The denominators are $2$ and $3$. Their least common multiple is $6$.

Multiply the entire equation by $6$:

$$
6\left(\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{3}\right)=6\cdot5
$$

Simplify:

$$
3x+2=30
$$

Then:

$$
3x=28
$$

and

$$
\boxed{x=\frac{28}{3}}
$$

## Equations with decimals

Equations containing decimal numbers can be transformed into equations without decimals by multiplying by a suitable power of $10$.

For example:

$$
0.5x+1.2=3.7
$$

Multiply by $10$:

$$
5x+12=37
$$

Subtract $12$:

$$
5x=25
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{x=5}
$$

## Number of solutions

An equation can have:

- one solution;
- no solution;
- infinitely many solutions.

### One solution

$$
2x+4=10
$$

It has one solution:

$$
x=3
$$

### No solution

Consider:

$$
2x+4=2x+7
$$

Subtract $2x$:

$$
4=7
$$

This is impossible.

Therefore, the equation has **no solution**.

### Infinitely many solutions

Consider:

$$
2x+4=2x+4
$$

Subtracting $2x$ gives:

$$
4=4
$$

The equality is always true.

Therefore, **every value of $x$ is a solution**.

## Solution and verification

A solution should be checked in the original equation.

For example:

$$
4x-6=10
$$

Solve:

$$
4x=16
$$

$$
x=4
$$

Check:

$$
4(4)-6=10
$$

$$
16-6=10
$$

The equality holds, so:

$$
\boxed{x=4}
$$

## Equivalent equations

Two equations are **equivalent** when they have exactly the same solutions.

For example:

$$
2x+6=10
$$

and

$$
2x=4
$$

are equivalent equations because both have the solution:

$$
x=2
$$

The transformations used to solve an equation must preserve its solutions.

## Graphical interpretation

An equation can also be interpreted using functions.

Consider:

$$
2x+3=11
$$

We can define the functions:

$$
f(x)=2x+3
$$

and

$$
g(x)=11
$$

Solving the equation means finding the value of $x$ for which:

$$
f(x)=g(x)
$$

In other words, we look for the point where the two graphs intersect.

## Equations and products

An equation can contain products of expressions.

For example:

$$
(x-2)(x+3)=0
$$

A product is zero when at least one of its factors is zero:

$$
x-2=0
$$

or

$$
x+3=0
$$

Therefore:

$$
x=2
$$

or

$$
x=-3
$$

The solutions are:

$$
\boxed{x=2,\quad x=-3}
$$

This principle is particularly important when solving polynomial equations.

## Polynomial equations

A polynomial equation has the form:

$$
P(x)=0
$$

where $P(x)$ is a polynomial.

For example:

$$
x^2-5x+6=0
$$

We can factor it:

$$
(x-2)(x-3)=0
$$

Therefore:

$$
x=2
\quad\text{or}\quad
x=3
$$

The solutions are:

$$
\boxed{x=2,\quad x=3}
$$

Quadratic equations are studied in more detail in the **quadratic equations** topic.

## Rational equations

In a rational equation, the unknown appears in one or more denominators.

For example:

$$
\frac{1}{x}=2
$$

Before solving, we must remember that a denominator cannot be zero:

$$
x\neq0
$$

Multiply by $x$:

$$
1=2x
$$

Therefore:

$$
x=\frac12
$$

The solution is valid because $\frac12\neq0$.

## Restrictions

Some equations have values that are not allowed.

For example:

$$
\frac{2}{x-3}=1
$$

The denominator cannot be zero:

$$
x-3\neq0
$$

so:

$$
x\neq3
$$

Now solve:

$$
2=x-3
$$

$$
x=5
$$

Since $5\neq3$, the solution is valid:

$$
\boxed{x=5}
$$

We must always check that the solutions satisfy the original restrictions.

## Common mistakes

When solving equations, avoid these mistakes:

- performing an operation on only one side;
- changing the sign of a term incorrectly;
- forgetting to expand parentheses correctly;
- clearing denominators without considering their restrictions;
- accepting solutions that do not satisfy the original equation;
- losing solutions through non-equivalent transformations.

The fundamental rule is:

> Every transformation must preserve the equality and the solutions of the equation.

## Notation

In this topic we will use:

- $x$ to represent an unknown;
- $=$ to indicate an equality;
- $\neq$ to indicate that two expressions are different;
- $\Rightarrow$ to indicate a consequence;
- $\Leftrightarrow$ to indicate equivalence;
- $x=a$ to highlight a solution.

For example:

$$
2x+4=10
\Rightarrow
2x=6
\Rightarrow
x=3
$$
