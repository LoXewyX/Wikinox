# Numbers

## 1. What is a number?

A **number** is a mathematical object that we use to represent quantities, positions, measurements, relationships, and results of operations.

For example:

$$
3,\quad 15,\quad -7,\quad \frac{2}{3},\quad \sqrt{2},\quad \pi
$$

They are all numbers, but they do not all belong to the same number set.

For example:

- $3$ is a natural number.
- $-7$ is an integer.
- $\frac{2}{3}$ is a rational number.
- $\sqrt{2}$ is an irrational number.
- $\pi$ is an irrational number.

To classify numbers, we use different **number sets**. These sets are progressively expanded so that we can represent increasingly different types of numbers.

The main relationship between them is:

$$
\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}
$$

The symbol $\subset$ means **“is contained in”** or **“is a subset of”**.

For example:

$$
\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}
$$

means that every natural number is also an integer.

Throughout this topic, we will study the main number sets, their properties, and the operations that we can perform with them.

---

## 2. Positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero

Real numbers can be placed on a number line.

Before comparing numbers, it is important to distinguish between **positive numbers**, **negative numbers**, and **zero**.

A number is **positive** when it is greater than zero.

The symbol $>$ means **“greater than”**.

For example:

$$
x>0
$$

means that the number $x$ is greater than zero.

Positive numbers include, for example:

$$
1,\quad4,\quad10,\quad25
$$

A number is **negative** when it is less than zero.

The symbol $<$ means **“less than”**.

For example:

$$
x<0
$$

means that the number $x$ is less than zero.

Some negative numbers are:

$$
-1,\quad-4,\quad-10,\quad-25
$$

The number $0$ is neither positive nor negative.

We can visualize these positions:

:::number-line{min="-5" max="5" highlight="-3,0,3"}
:::

On the number line:

- positive numbers are to the right of zero;
- negative numbers are to the left of zero;
- zero is between them.

For example:

$$
-3<0<3
$$

The sign $-$ in front of a number indicates its opposite.

For example:

$$
-3
$$

is the opposite of $3$.

Similarly:

$$
-(-3)=3
$$

The opposite of $0$ is $0$ itself:

$$
-0=0
$$

### Opposite numbers

Two numbers are **opposites** when they have the same absolute value and different signs.

For example, $3$ and $-3$ are opposites.

Likewise, $8$ and $-8$ are opposites.

In general, if $a$ is a number, its opposite is written as $-a$.

---

## 3. The number line

The **number line** allows us to represent numbers as points placed along a line.

Zero is usually used as a reference point.

:::number-line{min="-5" max="5"}
:::

A fundamental property of the number line is that numbers increase from left to right.

Therefore, if one number is farther to the right than another, it is greater.

For example:

$$
5>2
$$

because $5$ is to the right of $2$.

Similarly:

$$
-5<-2
$$

because $-5$ is to the left of $-2$.

This is especially important with negative numbers.

Although $5$ is greater than $2$, among negative numbers we have:

$$
-2>-5
$$

because $-2$ is farther to the right on the number line.

For example:

$$
-1>-4
$$

### Distance between numbers

The number line also allows us to interpret the **distance between two numbers**.

The distance between two points is always a non-negative quantity.

For example, the distance between $2$ and $7$ is:

$$
7-2=5
$$

The distance between $-3$ and $2$ is:

$$
2-(-3)=5
$$

Later we will see that we can express the distance between any two numbers $a$ and $b$ using absolute value:

$$
|a-b|
$$

## 4. Comparing numbers

To compare numbers, we use different mathematical symbols.

The main ones are:

| Symbol | Meaning                  |
| ------ | ------------------------ |
| $>$    | greater than             |
| $<$    | less than                |
| $=$    | equal to                 |
| $\geq$ | greater than or equal to |
| $\leq$ | less than or equal to    |
| $\neq$ | not equal to             |

The symbol $=$ means **“is equal to”**.

For example:

$$
5=5
$$

The symbol $\geq$ means **“greater than or equal to”**.

For example:

$$
x\geq3
$$

means that $x$ can be greater than $3$ or exactly equal to $3$.

The symbol $\leq$ means **“less than or equal to”**.

For example:

$$
x\leq3
$$

means that $x$ can be less than $3$ or exactly equal to $3$.

The symbol $\neq$ means **“not equal to”**.

For example:

$$
x\neq0
$$

means that $x$ is not equal to zero.

### Comparison on the number line

The position of numbers on the number line allows us to compare them.

If $a$ is to the left of $b$, then:

$$
a<b
$$

If $a$ is to the right of $b$, then:

$$
a>b
$$

## 5. Number sets

Numbers are classified into different sets.

The letters used to represent them are conventional mathematical notation.

The symbol $\mathbb{N}$ represents the set of **natural numbers**.

The symbol $\mathbb{Z}$ represents the set of **integers**.

The symbol $\mathbb{Q}$ represents the set of **rational numbers**.

The symbol $\mathbb{R}$ represents the set of **real numbers**.

The relationship between these sets is:

$$
\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}
$$

:::number-sets
:::

The symbol $\subset$ means that every element of the set on the left also belongs to the set on the right.

For example:

$$
\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}
$$

means that every integer is also a rational number.

In addition to these sets, we distinguish **irrational numbers**, which are real numbers that are not rational.

We will denote the set of irrational numbers by $I$.

Irrational numbers satisfy:

$$
I=\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
$$

Here the symbol $\setminus$ represents the **set difference**: $A\setminus B$ contains the elements that belong to $A$ but do not belong to $B$.

Therefore, $I$ contains the real numbers that are not rational.

---

## 6. Natural numbers

**Natural numbers** are mainly used for counting and ordering.

On this site, we will use the convention:

$$
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,3,4,\ldots\}
$$

The symbol $\in$ means **“belongs to”**.

For example:

$$
3\in\mathbb{N}
$$

means that $3$ belongs to the set of natural numbers.

The symbol $\ldots$ indicates that the sequence continues according to the same pattern.

Thus:

$$
0,1,2,3,4,\ldots
$$

continues with $5$, $6$, $7$, and so on.

Some books use a different convention and start the natural numbers at $1$:

$$
\{1,2,3,4,\ldots\}
$$

That is why it is important to check which convention is being used.

### Basic properties

Natural numbers are closed under addition and multiplication.

This means that adding or multiplying two natural numbers gives another natural number.

For example:

$$
3+5=8
$$

and:

$$
4\cdot6=24
$$

However, subtraction and division do not always produce natural numbers.

For example:

$$
3-5=-2
$$

and $-2$ does not belong to $\mathbb{N}$.

Also:

$$
1\div2=\frac{1}{2}
$$

and $\frac{1}{2}$ does not belong to $\mathbb{N}$.

---

## 7. Integers

**Integers** include the natural numbers, their opposites, and zero.

They are represented by $\mathbb{Z}$:

$$
\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
$$

For example:

$$
-5\in\mathbb{Z}
$$

because $-5$ is an integer.

Also:

$$
7\in\mathbb{Z}
$$

Natural numbers are contained in the integers:

$$
\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}
$$

For example:

$$
4\in\mathbb{N}
$$

and therefore:

$$
4\in\mathbb{Z}
$$

### Adding integers

To add integers, we can use the number line.

If we add a positive number, we move to the right.

If we add a negative number, we move to the left.

For example:

$$
3+(-5)=-2
$$

We start at $3$ and move five units to the left.

### Multiplying integers

When multiplying integers, we must consider their signs.

Two fundamental rules are:

$$
(+)\cdot(+)=+
$$

and:

$$
(-)\cdot(-)=+
$$

Whereas:

$$
(+)\cdot(-)=-
$$

and:

$$
(-)\cdot(+)=-
$$

In other words:

- equal signs produce a positive result;
- different signs produce a negative result.

For example:

$$
(-3)\cdot(-4)=12
$$

and:

$$
(-3)\cdot4=-12
$$

---

## 8. Absolute value

The **absolute value** of a number is its distance from zero on the number line.

Absolute value is represented by two vertical bars:

$$
|x|
$$

The bars $|\,|$ indicate that we are calculating the absolute value of $x$.

For example:

$$
|-5|=5
$$

because $-5$ is a distance of $5$ units from zero.

Also:

$$
|5|=5
$$

Both $-5$ and $5$ are five units away from zero.

:::absolute-value
:::

Absolute value is never negative.

Therefore:

$$
|x|\geq0
$$

The symbol $\geq$ means **“greater than or equal to”**.

### Distance between two numbers

Absolute value allows us to express the distance between two numbers $a$ and $b$:

$$
|a-b|
$$

For example, the distance between $-3$ and $4$ is:

$$
|-3-4|=|-7|=7
$$

Therefore, the numbers $-3$ and $4$ are seven units apart.

---

## 9. Rational numbers

A **rational number** is any number that can be written as a fraction:

$$
\frac{a}{b}
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are integers and $b\neq0$.

The symbol $\neq$ means **“not equal to”**.

Therefore, the denominator $b$ cannot be zero.

For example:

$$
\frac{3}{4}
$$

is a rational number because $3$ and $4$ are integers and $4\neq0$.

Other rational numbers are:

$$
\frac{-5}{2},\quad
\frac{7}{3},\quad
8
$$

The integer $8$ can also be written as a fraction:

$$
8=\frac{8}{1}
$$

Therefore, every integer is rational:

$$
\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}
$$

### Equivalent fractions

Two fractions can represent the same number.

For example:

$$
\frac{1}{2}=\frac{2}{4}=\frac{3}{6}
$$

These fractions are **equivalent**.

A fraction can be simplified by dividing the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number.

For example:

$$
\frac{6}{8}=\frac{3}{4}
$$

---

## 10. Decimal representation of rational numbers

Rational numbers can be expressed using decimals.

For example:

$$
\frac{1}{2}=0.5
$$

and:

$$
\frac{3}{4}=0.75
$$

The decimal representation of a rational number can be:

- finite;
- infinite and repeating.

A decimal representation is **repeating** when a sequence of digits repeats indefinitely.

For example:

$$
\frac{1}{3}=0.333\ldots
$$

The pattern $3$ repeats indefinitely.

Another example is:

$$
\frac{2}{11}=0.181818\ldots
$$

In this case, the pattern $18$ repeats.

Therefore, every rational number has a finite or repeating decimal representation.

---

## 11. Irrational numbers

**Irrational numbers** are real numbers that cannot be expressed as a fraction of two integers with a non-zero denominator.

In other words, an irrational number is a real number that does not belong to $\mathbb{Q}$.

The expression:

$$
I=\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
$$

uses $\setminus$ to indicate the set difference.

Some examples of irrational numbers are:

$$
\sqrt{2},\quad\sqrt{3},\quad\pi
$$

Their decimal representations are infinite and non-repeating.

For example:

$$
\sqrt{2}=1.41421356\ldots
$$

The digits continue indefinitely and do not follow a repeating pattern.

It is important to distinguish between an infinite decimal representation and an irrational decimal representation: not every infinite decimal is necessarily irrational. For example:

$$
\frac{1}{3}=0.333\ldots
$$

is infinite, but it is rational because its digits repeat periodically.

## 12. Fractions

A **fraction** represents a quantity made up of equal parts of a unit.

A fraction is written as:

$$
\frac{a}{b}
$$

The number on top is called the **numerator** and represents how many parts we take.

The number on the bottom is called the **denominator** and represents how many equal parts each unit is divided into.

For example:

$$
\frac{3}{5}
$$

In this fraction, $3$ is the numerator and $5$ is the denominator. Therefore, we divide one unit into 5 equal parts and take 3 of them.

:::fraction{numerator="3" denominator="5"}
:::

### Proper fractions

A **proper fraction** has a numerator smaller than its denominator. Its value is less than 1.

For example:

$$
\frac{3}{5}
$$

The unit is divided into 5 equal parts and 3 are taken.

:::fraction{numerator="3" denominator="5"}
:::

Another example is:

$$
\frac{7}{10}
$$

The unit is divided into 10 equal parts and 7 are taken.

:::fraction{numerator="7" denominator="10"}
:::

### Fractions equal to one unit

When the numerator and denominator are equal, the fraction represents exactly one unit.

For example:

$$
\frac{4}{4}=1
$$

The denominator indicates that each unit has 4 parts, and the numerator indicates that we take all 4 parts.

:::fraction{numerator="4" denominator="4"}
:::

### Improper fractions

An **improper fraction** has a numerator greater than its denominator. Its value is greater than 1.

For example:

$$
\frac{7}{4}
$$

The denominator $4$ indicates that each unit is made up of 4 equal parts.

We have 7 parts. The first 4 form one complete unit, leaving 3 parts of a second unit.

Therefore:

$$
\frac{7}{4}=1+\frac{3}{4}
$$

We can also write this quantity as a mixed number:

$$
1\frac{3}{4}
$$

:::fraction{numerator="7" denominator="4"}
:::

Another example is:

$$
\frac{9}{4}
$$

We can form two complete units using 8 parts, with 1 part remaining from a third unit:

$$
\frac{9}{4}=2+\frac{1}{4}
$$

:::fraction{numerator="9" denominator="4"}
:::

Similarly:

$$
\frac{11}{5}=2+\frac{1}{5}
$$

:::fraction{numerator="11" denominator="5"}
:::

### Equivalent fractions

Two fractions are **equivalent** when they represent the same quantity, even though they are written with different numbers.

For example:

$$
\frac{1}{2}=\frac{2}{4}=\frac{3}{6}
$$

All three fractions represent half of one unit.

:::fraction{numerator="1" denominator="2"}
:::

:::fraction{numerator="2" denominator="4"}
:::

:::fraction{numerator="3" denominator="6"}
:::

We can obtain an equivalent fraction by multiplying the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number.

For example:

$$
\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1\cdot2}{2\cdot2}=\frac{2}{4}
$$

We can also simplify a fraction by dividing the numerator and denominator by the same common divisor.

For example:

$$
\frac{6}{8}=\frac{3}{4}
$$

because both 6 and 8 can be divided by 2.

:::fraction{numerator="6" denominator="8"}
:::

:::fraction{numerator="3" denominator="4"}
:::

### Fractions greater than one

A fraction can represent a quantity made up of several complete units and part of another unit.

For example:

$$
\frac{13}{5}
$$

Since each unit contains 5 parts, we can form two complete units using 10 parts, leaving 3 parts:

$$
\frac{13}{5}=2+\frac{3}{5}
$$

:::fraction{numerator="13" denominator="5"}
:::

This allows us to interpret an improper fraction as the sum of a whole number and a proper fraction.

In general, if the numerator is greater than the denominator, we can divide the numerator by the denominator to obtain the complete units and the remaining parts.

For example:

$$
\frac{17}{6}
$$

We have 2 complete units because $2\cdot6=12$, with 5 parts remaining:

$$
\frac{17}{6}=2+\frac{5}{6}
$$

:::fraction{numerator="17" denominator="6"}
:::

## 13. Operations with fractions

### Addition and subtraction with the same denominator

If two fractions have the same denominator, we can add or subtract their numerators while keeping the denominator.

For example:

$$
\frac{2}{7}+\frac{3}{7}
=
\frac{5}{7}
$$

And:

$$
\frac{6}{7}-\frac{2}{7}
=
\frac{4}{7}
$$

### Addition and subtraction with different denominators

When the denominators are different, we first need to find a common denominator.

For example:

$$
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}
$$

The least common multiple of $2$ and $3$ is $6$.

Therefore:

$$
\frac{1}{2}=\frac{3}{6}
$$

and:

$$
\frac{1}{3}=\frac{2}{6}
$$

Then:

$$
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}
=
\frac{3}{6}+\frac{2}{6}
=
\frac{5}{6}
$$

### Multiplication

To multiply fractions, we multiply the numerators together and the denominators together.

For example:

$$
\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{4}{5}
=
\frac{8}{15}
$$

### Division

Dividing by a fraction is equivalent to multiplying by its reciprocal.

The **reciprocal** of $\frac{a}{b}$ is $\frac{b}{a}$, provided that $a\neq0$.

For example:

$$
\frac{2}{3}\div\frac{4}{5}
=
\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{5}{4}
=
\frac{10}{12}
=
\frac{5}{6}
$$

## 14. Powers

A **power** is a way of representing repeated multiplication in a compact form.

The expression:

$$
a^n
$$

is read as **“a raised to the power n”**.

The number $a$ is the **base** and the number $n$ is the **exponent**.

If $n$ is a positive natural number, the power means multiplying the base by itself $n$ times.

For example:

$$
2^4=2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2=16
$$

Here:

- $2$ is the base;
- $4$ is the exponent;
- $16$ is the result.

### Zero exponent

For any non-zero number:

$$
a^0=1
$$

For example:

$$
5^0=1
$$

### Negative exponents

If $a\neq0$ and $n$ is positive:

$$
a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}
$$

For example:

$$
2^{-3}=\frac{1}{2^3}=\frac{1}{8}
$$

---

## 15. Properties of powers

Powers satisfy several important properties.

### Product of powers with the same base

When multiplying powers with the same base, we add their exponents:

$$
a^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}
$$

For example:

$$
2^3\cdot2^2=2^5
$$

### Quotient of powers with the same base

When dividing powers with the same base, we subtract their exponents:

$$
\frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}
$$

provided that $a\neq0$.

For example:

$$
\frac{2^5}{2^2}=2^3
$$

### Power of a power

When a power is raised to another power, we multiply the exponents:

$$
(a^m)^n=a^{m\cdot n}
$$

For example:

$$
(2^3)^2=2^6
$$

---

## 16. Square roots

The symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ is called the **radical sign**.

The expression:

$$
\sqrt{a}
$$

represents the **principal square root** of $a$.

For $a\geq0$, $\sqrt{a}$ is the non-negative number whose square is $a$.

The symbol $\geq$ means **“greater than or equal to”**.

For example:

$$
\sqrt{9}=3
$$

because:

$$
3^2=9
$$

and $3$ is the non-negative number whose square is $9$.

Equivalently:

$$
\sqrt{a}=b
$$

if and only if:

$$
b\geq0
\quad\text{and}\quad
b^2=a
$$

For example:

$$
\sqrt{16}=4
$$

We should not write $\sqrt{16}=\pm4$.

The principal square root is $4$.

However, the equation:

$$
x^2=16
$$

has two solutions:

$$
x=4
\quad\text{or}\quad
x=-4
$$

---

## 17. Roots and powers

Roots and powers are related.

For example:

$$
\sqrt{a}=a^{1/2}
$$

for $a\geq0$.

Also:

$$
\sqrt[n]{a}=a^{1/n}
$$

when the expression is defined.

The number $n$ indicates the **index of the root**.

For example:

$$
\sqrt[3]{8}=2
$$

because:

$$
2^3=8
$$

The cube root can also be defined for negative numbers:

$$
\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2
$$

because:

$$
(-2)^3=-8
$$

---

## 18. Order of operations

When an expression contains several operations, we must follow a specific order.

The usual order is:

1. parentheses;
2. powers and roots;
3. multiplication and division;
4. addition and subtraction.

For example:

$$
2+3\cdot4
$$

We first perform the multiplication:

$$
3\cdot4=12
$$

and then the addition:

$$
2+12=14
$$

Therefore:

$$
2+3\cdot4=14
$$

Parentheses allow us to change this order.

For example:

$$
(2+3)\cdot4
$$

First we calculate the expression inside the parentheses:

$$
2+3=5
$$

and then:

$$
5\cdot4=20
$$

Therefore:

$$
(2+3)\cdot4=20
$$

## 19. Divisibility

We say that an integer $a$ is **divisible** by another integer $b$ if there is an integer $k$ such that:

$$
a=b\cdot k
$$

To express that $b$ divides $a$, we use the symbol $\mid$.

The expression:

$$
b\mid a
$$

is read **“b divides a”**.

For example:

$$
3\mid12
$$

because there is an integer $4$ such that:

$$
12=3\cdot4
$$

If a number does not divide another number, we can use the symbol $\nmid$, which means **“does not divide”**.

For example:

$$
5\nmid12
$$

because there is no integer $k$ such that:

$$
12=5\cdot k
$$

### Multiples

Multiples of a number are obtained by multiplying it by integers.

For example, some multiples of $4$ are:

$$
0,\quad4,\quad8,\quad12,\quad16,\ldots
$$

---

## 20. Prime numbers

A natural number greater than $1$ is **prime** if it has exactly two distinct positive divisors: $1$ and the number itself.

For example, $7$ is prime because its only positive divisors are $1$ and $7$.

In contrast, $8$ is not prime because it has more divisors:

$$
1,\quad2,\quad4,\quad8
$$

Some prime numbers are:

$$
2,\quad3,\quad5,\quad7,\quad11,\quad13,\quad17,\ldots
$$

The number $2$ is the only prime number that is even.

A natural number greater than $1$ that is not prime is called **composite**.

---

## 21. Prime factorization

Every natural number greater than $1$ can be expressed as a product of prime numbers.

This expression is called its **prime factorization**.

For example:

$$
60=2\cdot2\cdot3\cdot5
$$

We can also write:

$$
60=2^2\cdot3\cdot5
$$

Prime factorization is useful for simplifying fractions and calculating the greatest common divisor and least common multiple.

---

## 22. Greatest common divisor

The **greatest common divisor**, abbreviated as **GCD**, of several numbers is the greatest natural number that divides all of them exactly.

For example, consider $12$ and $18$.

Their common divisors are:

$$
1,\quad2,\quad3,\quad6
$$

The greatest one is $6$.

Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{GCD}(12,18)=6
$$

The expression $\operatorname{GCD}$ means **greatest common divisor**.

### Calculation using prime factors

We can factor:

$$
12=2^2\cdot3
$$

and:

$$
18=2\cdot3^2
$$

The common factors are $2$ and $3$.

We take each common factor with the smallest exponent:

$$
\operatorname{GCD}(12,18)=2\cdot3=6
$$

---

## 23. Least common multiple

The **least common multiple**, abbreviated as **LCM**, of several numbers is the smallest positive natural number that is a multiple of all of them.

For example, the positive multiples of $4$ are:

$$
4,\quad8,\quad12,\quad16,\ldots
$$

and some positive multiples of $6$ are:

$$
6,\quad12,\quad18,\quad24,\ldots
$$

The first positive multiple they have in common is $12$.

Therefore:

$$
\operatorname{LCM}(4,6)=12
$$

The expression $\operatorname{LCM}$ means **least common multiple**.

### Calculation using prime factors

We have:

$$
4=2^2
$$

and:

$$
6=2\cdot3
$$

To calculate the LCM, we take every prime factor that appears, using the greatest exponent in each case:

$$
\operatorname{LCM}(4,6)=2^2\cdot3=12
$$

---

## 24. Properties of addition

Addition of real numbers satisfies several important properties.

### Commutative property

The **commutative property** means that we can change the order of the addends without changing the result.

$$
a+b=b+a
$$

For example:

$$
3+5=5+3
$$

### Associative property

The **associative property** means that we can change the grouping of the addends without changing the result.

$$
(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)
$$

For example:

$$
(2+3)+4=2+(3+4)
$$

In both cases we obtain $9$.

### Identity element

$0$ is the **identity element for addition** because adding zero does not change a number:

$$
a+0=a
$$

### Additive inverse

Every real number $a$ has an opposite, $-a$.

Their sum is zero:

$$
a+(-a)=0
$$

---

## 25. Properties of multiplication

Multiplication also has fundamental properties.

### Commutative property

We can change the order of the factors:

$$
a\cdot b=b\cdot a
$$

For example:

$$
3\cdot5=5\cdot3
$$

### Associative property

We can change the grouping of the factors:

$$
(a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c)
$$

### Identity element

$1$ is the identity element for multiplication:

$$
a\cdot1=a
$$

### Distributive property

Multiplication distributes over addition:

$$
a(b+c)=a\cdot b+a\cdot c
$$

For example:

$$
3(4+5)=3\cdot4+3\cdot5
$$

Calculating:

$$
3\cdot9=12+15
$$

and:

$$
27=27
$$

## 26. Percentages

A **percentage** represents a quantity expressed **per hundred**.

The symbol $\%$ is read **“percent”**.

For example:

$$
25\%
$$

means **25 out of every 100**.

Therefore:

$$
25\%=\frac{25}{100}=\frac{1}{4}
$$

To calculate a percentage of a quantity, we can convert the percentage into a fraction or decimal.

For example, $20\%$ of $50$ is:

$$
20\%\cdot50
=
\frac{20}{100}\cdot50
=
10
$$

Therefore, $20\%$ of $50$ is $10$.

### Percentage increases and decreases

If a quantity increases by $10\%$, we add one tenth of the original quantity.

If a quantity decreases by $10\%$, we subtract one tenth of the original quantity.

For example, increasing $100$ by $10\%$ gives:

$$
100+10=110
$$

## 27. Scientific notation

**Scientific notation** allows us to write very large or very small numbers in a compact form.

A number in scientific notation has the form:

$$
a\cdot10^n
$$

where $a$ is a number whose absolute value is greater than or equal to $1$ and less than $10$, and $n$ is an integer.

For example:

$$
3000000=3\cdot10^6
$$

Also:

$$
0.000004=4\cdot10^{-6}
$$

Positive exponents allow us to represent large numbers.

Negative exponents allow us to represent small numbers.

## 28. Real numbers

**Real numbers** include all rational and irrational numbers.

They are represented by the symbol $\mathbb{R}$.

Therefore:

$$
\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}
$$

and also:

$$
I\subset\mathbb{R}
$$

Real numbers can be represented by points on a number line.

Within the real numbers we find:

- natural numbers;
- integers;
- rational numbers;
- irrational numbers.

The complete relationship can be visualized as:

:::number-sets
:::

For example:

$$
-3,\quad0,\quad\frac{1}{2},\quad\sqrt{2},\quad\pi
$$

are all real numbers.

However, they belong to different categories.

For example:

$$
-3\in\mathbb{Z}
$$

while:

$$
\frac{1}{2}\in\mathbb{Q}
$$

and:

$$
\sqrt{2}\in I
$$

## 29. Intervals of real numbers

An **interval** is a set of real numbers between specified boundaries.

To describe intervals, we use parentheses and square brackets.

A square bracket $[$ or $]$ indicates that the endpoint **is included**.

A parenthesis $($ or $)$ indicates that the endpoint **is not included**.

For example:

$$
[2,5]
$$

represents all real numbers from $2$ to $5$, including both endpoints.

This means:

$$
2\leq x\leq5
$$

The symbol $\leq$ means **“less than or equal to”**.

Therefore, $2$ and $5$ belong to the interval.

### Open interval

The interval:

$$
(2,5)
$$

does not include the endpoints.

Therefore:

$$
2<x<5
$$

The symbol $<$ means **“less than”**.

Thus, $3$ belongs to the interval, but $2$ and $5$ do not.

### Half-open intervals

We can also include only one of the endpoints.

For example:

$$
[2,5)
$$

includes $2$ but does not include $5$.

It can be expressed as:

$$
2\leq x<5
$$

Another example is:

$$
(2,5]
$$

which is expressed as:

$$
2<x\leq5
$$

### Infinite intervals

We can also represent sets that extend indefinitely.

For example:

$$
[3,\infty)
$$

represents all real numbers greater than or equal to $3$.

The symbol $\infty$ represents the concept of **infinity**. It is not a real number, but a way of indicating that the interval continues without bound.

We can also write:

$$
(-\infty,4]
$$

to represent all real numbers less than or equal to $4$.

## 30. Union and intersection of sets

When working with sets, we may need to combine or compare different sets.

The symbol $\cup$ represents the **union** of two sets.

The union contains all elements that belong to the first set, the second set, or both.

For example:

$$
[1,3]\cup[5,7]
$$

represents all numbers between $1$ and $3$, together with all numbers between $5$ and $7$.

The symbol $\cap$ represents the **intersection** of two sets.

The intersection contains only the elements that belong to both sets.

For example:

$$
[1,5]\cap[3,7]=[3,5]
$$

because the numbers that belong to both intervals are those between $3$ and $5$.

The symbol $\varnothing$ represents the **empty set**, that is, a set that contains no elements.

For example:

$$
[1,2]\cap[3,4]=\varnothing
$$

because there is no real number that belongs to both intervals.

## 31. Relationships between number sets

Number sets are not independent.

Each set contains smaller sets.

The fundamental relationship is:

$$
\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}
$$

This means:

- every natural number is an integer;
- every integer is rational;
- every rational number is real.

Irrational numbers also belong to the real numbers:

$$
I\subset\mathbb{R}
$$

But irrational numbers are not rational:

$$
I\cap\mathbb{Q}=\varnothing
$$

Here $\cap$ represents the intersection and $\varnothing$ represents the empty set.

Therefore, the real numbers can be divided into two large groups:

$$
\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{Q}\cup I
$$

The symbol $\cup$ represents the union.

This classification will be fundamental for subsequently studying algebraic expressions, functions, equations, and calculus.
